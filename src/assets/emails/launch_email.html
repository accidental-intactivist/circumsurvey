In America, people are raised with a very specific story about infant circumcision. They are assured it is a minor, harmless hygiene upgrade. They are told that because it happens to a baby, he won’t remember it, and therefore, he won’t care. Because that story is repeated so often, most people think the issue is settled.

And if you look closely, the medical justifications have functioned as a rotating "Disease of the Decade" for 150 years. They shift from masturbation and epilepsy to syphilis, then cervical cancer, to UTIs, and eventually HIV. The goalposts constantly move, and the proposed "cure" magically remains exactly the same: the surgical removal of healthy, highly-innervated tissue from an infant.

Today, that cultural momentum is finally fracturing. Routine infant circumcision is no longer recommended as a preventative health measure by any national or international medical authority in the world. In the United States, newborn rates have fallen below 50% for the first time in over a century. The architects of the very policies that sustained the practice are now issuing unprecedented retractions.

My parents made an unusual choice in the 1970s: they left me intact. Growing up, it gave me an outsider's lens to look at a routine procedure and ask: Why isn't this weird to more people?

So what happens when you bypass a collapsing medical consensus, cut through the cultural noise, and simply ask the adult men living in these bodies what actually goes on down there?

I didn't have an advertising budget or a clinical recruitment pipeline. I just put out a grassroots survey. And something incredible happened: 500 of you showed up.

Now, I finally have the data compiled.
